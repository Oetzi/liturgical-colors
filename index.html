Color Meanings

What each liturgical color signifies
  • Green Hope and growth

    Worn through Ordinary Time, the longest stretch of the year. It is the color of living things, for the steady, everyday growth of faith between the great seasons.

  • White or Gold Joy and glory

    Worn in Christmas and Easter Time, on feasts of the Lord (other than of his Passion), of Mary, the angels, and saints who were not martyrs. It stands for light, purity and the Resurrection.

  • Red Blood and fire

    Worn on Palm Sunday, Good Friday and Pentecost, and on feasts of the apostles and martyrs. It recalls the blood of Christ and those who died for the faith, and the fire of the Holy Spirit.

  • Violet (Purple) Penance and preparation

    Worn in Advent and Lent, the seasons of preparation for Christmas and Easter. It may also be worn at Masses for the dead.

  • Rose Joy within a season of waiting

    Worn on only two days: Gaudete Sunday (3rd of Advent) and Laetare Sunday (4th of Lent). Both names mean “rejoice” — the violet lightens because the feast is close.

  • Black Mourning

    An option for All Souls’ Day and funeral Masses. Violet or white is more often seen today, but black keeps its place as the traditional color of mourning.

Where the Colors Come From

A short history
  1. Early centuries. There was no fixed scheme. Vestments were simply good clothing, most often white.
  2. Around 1200. Pope Innocent III, in his treatise on the Mass (De sacro altaris mysterio), described the Roman custom of his day: white for feasts, red for martyrs and Pentecost, black for penance and mourning, green for ordinary days, with violet as a variant of black.
  3. 1570. The Roman Missal of Pope St. Pius V made the sequence of white, red, green, violet and black the general rule for the Roman Rite.
  4. Rose. A lightened violet became the custom for Gaudete and Laetare Sundays, the midpoints of Advent and Lent.
  5. Today. The General Instruction of the Roman Missal (no. 346) sets the colors now in use. In the United States, violet, white or black may be worn at Masses for the dead.

The calendar here follows the Ordinary Form for the dioceses of the United States. Your own diocese or parish may keep local feasts that are not shown.
